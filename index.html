Payroll SLA Guide for UK Employers: Clear Pay, Reporting and Support

A payroll service level agreement template gives UK employers a clear written record of how pay will be prepared, checked, approved and reported each month. It names who does what and how errors will be corrected.

What a Payroll SLA Is and Why It Matters

A payroll SLA is a written agreement between an employer and the team or provider that runs payroll. It describes the work in plain terms, names the people responsible for each step, and records the standards that pay must meet. It is not a contract for the sake of paperwork. It is a working tool that helps everyone know what good looks like before problems arise.

Without a clear agreement, assumptions fill the gaps. Managers may send late changes and expect same day processing. Payroll staff may wait for approvals that never arrive. Employees may hear different answers from different people.

How to Use This Guide and Template in Practice

Start with the real pay calendar for your business. List every pay date, every group of employees, and every source of pay information. Then work backwards from each pay date and agree the last safe time for changes, the approval point, and the time needed for checks. That simple exercise often reveals where delays begin.

Next, agree the template sections with the people who will live with them. Include payroll, finance, human resources and managers who approve overtime or absence. Ask each group what they need, what they can provide, and where they need help. A template agreed in isolation rarely survives the first busy month.

  • Use one master copy and record changes with a date.
  • Keep pay dates beside the main agreement.
  • Review the agreement after three pay runs.
  • Train new approvers before their first approval.

Core Details Every Agreement Should Record

Every agreement should start with the basics, recorded in full and kept up to date. That includes the legal names of the parties, the payroll groups covered, the pay frequencies, the pay dates, and the main contacts on each side. It should also record the systems used, the way data will be sent, and the format required for each file.

  • Employer and provider names, addresses and main contacts.
  • Pay groups, pay frequencies and agreed pay dates.
  • Systems, file formats and secure transfer routes.
  • Scope of work, including what is excluded.
  • Review dates and the way changes will be agreed.

Scope matters most. State clearly whether the work covers starter and leaver processing, pensions, statutory pay, expenses, salary sacrifice, bonuses, court orders and year end reporting. If a task sits outside scope, say so in writing and agree how it will be handled if it arises. Vague scope creates the most common disputes in payroll support.

Pay Calendar, Cut Offs and the Approval Flow

The pay calendar is the heart of the agreement. It should show the cut off date for each pay run, the date for manager approval, the date for payroll checks, the payday, and the date for HMRC submissions. Each date should have a named owner. A calendar without owners is only a wish list.

Agree what counts as a late change and how late changes will be treated. Some changes can be included with extra checks. Others may need to wait for the next pay run or be paid as a separate payment. Record the rule, the approval needed, and the way employees will be told. That protects trust when a deadline is missed for a fair reason.

Accuracy Checks and Quality Controls That Build Trust

Accuracy is built by checks, not by hope. The agreement should list the checks that will happen before every pay run and who will complete each check. That list should cover new starters, leavers, changes to pay, overtime, absence, statutory pay, pension deductions and tax codes. Each check should leave a trace that can be reviewed later.

Agree how errors found before payday will be fixed and how errors found after payday will be handled. Record the correction window, the approval needed for a correction, and the way affected employees will be told. Employees should hear about a correction from the employer first, in clear language, with the new pay date and the reason in simple terms.

Evidence beats memory. Ask for a short check record for each pay run. It should show what was checked, by whom, and what was found.

HMRC Reporting, FPS, EPS and Year End Duties

HMRC reporting must be accurate and on time. The agreement should state who prepares the Full Payment Submission, who checks it, and who submits it. It should also state who prepares an Employer Payment Summary when one is needed, and who checks year end forms and statements. Each duty needs a named owner and a named deputy.

Agree the timetable for submissions in relation to payday and record what happens if payroll data arrives late. State how tax codes, student loan notices, court orders and starter declarations will be applied.

Pension, Statutory Pay and Employee Deductions

Pension work needs precision. The agreement should record the pension schemes used, the worker groups in each scheme, the contribution basis, and the dates for assessment, enrolment and opt out handling. It should state who checks pension deductions, who sends data to the pension provider, and who reconciles payments.

Statutory pay also needs clear rules. Record how Statutory Sick Pay, Statutory Maternity Pay, Statutory Paternity Pay, Shared Parental Pay and Statutory Adoption Pay will be calculated, evidenced and recorded. Agree what evidence managers must provide, how quickly it must arrive, and how queries about entitlement will be handled. Sensitivity matters here, so agree the tone and privacy of all messages to employees.

Data Protection, GDPR and Security Standards

Payroll data is highly sensitive. The agreement must state the legal basis for processing, the categories of data involved, and the roles of each party under UK data protection law. It should record who is the controller, who is the processor where relevant, and how instructions for processing will be given and recorded. If you are unsure of a role, check the wording with appropriate advisers before you sign.

Security rules should be practical and specific. Agree the secure routes for sending data, the access controls for payroll systems, the rules for passwords and multi factor checks, and the way leavers lose access. Record how data will be stored, how long it will be kept, and how it will be deleted securely at the end of the retention period. Never send payroll data by unsecured email.

Communication, Support and Escalation Routes

Good communication prevents most payroll complaints. The agreement should name a main contact on each side, a deputy for absence, and the best route for routine questions. It should state working hours for support, the way urgent issues will be flagged, and the way out of hours issues will be handled where relevant.

Escalation should be simple. Agree three levels of issue, what counts as urgent, and who will decide the priority. Record how an escalation will be logged, who will own it, and how the employee or manager will be kept informed. Silence during an escalation damages trust faster than the original error.

Corrections, Off Cycle Pay and Exception Handling

Even with strong checks, corrections will be needed. The agreement should define a correction, the window for reporting it, and the evidence required. It should state who approves a correction, how the employee will be told, and when the corrected pay will be made. Speed matters, but accuracy matters more.

Off cycle pay should be used sparingly and with clear rules. Agree when a separate payment is allowed, who must approve it, and how it will be reported to HMRC and in pension records. Record the cut off for an off cycle payment and the way the payment will be shown on the payslip.

Pricing, Scope Changes and Service Exceptions

Pricing should be transparent. The agreement should state what is included in the agreed fee, what is charged separately, and how charges for extra work will be quoted and approved. It should state the billing period, the information shown on each invoice, and the way queries about invoices will be handled. No extra charge should appear without prior agreement in writing.

Scope changes should follow a clear route. If the employer adds a new pay group, a new site, or a new type of payment, agree the impact on work, timing and fees before the change starts. Record the change, the date it takes effect, and the checks that will apply. That discipline protects both sides when the business grows or changes shape.

Watch for silent scope growth. If extra work becomes routine, move it into scope properly with agreed time and checks, rather than handling it as an exception every month.

Continuity Cover, Staffing and Service Resilience

Payroll must continue when people are away. The agreement should name the lead payroll contact, the trained deputy, and the way knowledge will be shared. It should state how handover notes will be kept, how access will be managed during absence, and how urgent work will be covered. No critical payroll task should depend on one person alone.

Resilience also covers systems and data. Agree how payroll data will be backed up, how access will be restored after an outage, and how a missed submission will be recovered. Keep a short continuity plan beside the main agreement and test it at least once a year.

Exit, Handover and Record Retention

Every agreement should plan for its own end. State the notice period, the form notice must take, and the duties that continue after notice is given. Agree the handover timetable, the data and records to be transferred, and the format for each file. A clear exit plan protects employees even when a commercial relationship ends.

Handover should be orderly and complete. List the payroll history, tax records, pension records, statutory pay history, court orders and employee data that must transfer. Agree who will check the transferred data, how errors in transfer will be fixed, and how long the outgoing provider will remain available for questions. Record retention duties should continue as agreed and as required by law.

Example Wording You Can Adapt in Plain English

The examples below show the tone to aim for. They are starting points for discussion, not legal advice. Adapt the wording to your business, your pay groups and your systems, and check important wording with appropriate advisers before you sign.

Payroll data for each pay run will be sent by the agreed cut off time in the agreed format. Late data will be logged and the employer contact will be told the same working day.

Pay will be checked for starters, leavers, pay changes, overtime, absence and statutory pay before approval. The approver will see a summary of totals and a list of unusual payments before pay is released.

Corrections will be agreed in writing, approved by the named approver, and told to the affected employee before the corrected pay date where possible.

Negotiation Checklist for UK Employers

Use this checklist in your negotiation meeting. Tick each item only when the wording is clear, the owner is named, and the date is agreed. Do not sign until every item has an answer in writing.

  • Pay dates and approval owners are listed.
  • Starter and leaver steps are described clearly.
  • Accuracy checks are agreed for every pay run.
  • HMRC and pension duties have named deputies.
  • Data rules and retention periods are recorded.
  • Support contacts and escalation routes are named.
  • Correction and off cycle pay rules are clear.
  • Pricing and scope change rules are transparent.
  • Continuity cover and backup routes are tested.
  • Exit and handover duties are agreed in advance.

Red Flags to Spot Before You Sign

Some warning signs deserve a pause. If the draft avoids naming owners, avoids dates, or promises outcomes without describing checks, ask for a rewrite. If data security is described only in general terms, ask for the specific routes, controls and retention rules.

  • Pay dates are missing or vague.
  • Approval has no evidence to review.
  • Corrections have no owner or message plan.
  • Extra charges need no prior agreement.
  • Exit duties are left to be agreed later.
  • Data is sent by unsecured email.

Questions to Ask a Payroll Provider

Ask open questions and listen for process answers, not slogans. Good answers name people, dates, checks and records. Vague answers suggest the process is not yet settled.

  • Who runs our pay and who is the deputy?
  • What checks happen before approval?
  • How is late data handled?
  • Who checks HMRC submissions and payments?
  • How are pension deductions reconciled?
  • How is payroll data stored securely?
  • What happens after a payday correction?
  • How is service quality reviewed?
  • What is included in the fee?
  • What handover happens if service ends?

Final Thoughts

A clear payroll agreement is an act of care for employees. It shows that pay will be handled with attention, that questions will be answered, and that mistakes will be fixed openly. It also gives payroll, finance and human resources teams a shared plan they can rely on in busy months.

Start with dates and owners, add checks and communication routes, and finish with data, pricing and exit duties. Review the agreement regularly and update it when the business changes. A living agreement, used every month, will serve the business far better than a perfect document left in a drawer.
